## Why thresholds grade only iowait and the probe (pattern:67)

- iowait has two measured bands: healthy 5–25%, failing 80–95%. Amber at 35 sits 10 points above the healthy max. Red at 60 sits 20 points below the failing min.
- The probe has one measured failure: a 1-row UPDATE took 17–19 s, and PostgREST returned 503/504. Healthy today is 0.54 s. Amber is 3 s. Red is 10 s, which equals pg_net's timeout, so a timeout lands as red by construction.
- Swap used cannot separate healthy from failing: it read 500–600 MB all day, including hours that were healthy. MemAvailable and swap-in rate have **no measured failing value**, so they are stored and not graded. Once the table holds a failing episode, a follow-up can grade them from evidence. Grading them now would be an invented knob.
- Thresholds are data (`runner_settings.db_health_thresholds`), stated in register row M6-14. I did not add an `OD-45` row: M6-14 is the written law for them, and `ses-234` pins OD-01..44.

## Why pg_cron inside the database, not a routine

- "Every 5 minutes, day and night" needs an always-on executor. The cloud runner fires every 3 h. GitHub Actions cron is best-effort, and the PAT cannot push workflows. Local scheduled tasks are retired. pg_cron is the only 5-minute clock the platform owns.
- The obvious objection: the reader lives inside the thing it measures. It fails in the safe direction. A thrashing database stops producing readings, the newest reading ages past 15 min, `db_health_level()` returns `unsafe`, and both gates refuse (pattern:165: a sensor must carry its age and fail closed). When the database comes back, the first reading files `db-health-readings-gap` with the gap length, so the outage reaches the findings list even though nothing could be written during it.
- Load is small. Every 5 min: two HTTP calls, one row, and response rows deleted right away. That avoids 6 h × 169 KB ≈ 12 MB of `net._http_response` bloat on a 0.5 GB server. An advisory lock stops overlapping ticks, and a 60 s statement timeout caps each tick.

## Why 15-min hysteresis on the gate

The ticket asks to resume only after 15 min green, which the (d) sentence states. The gate grades the worst level in the last `window_minutes` readings, so one green reading after a red does not reopen starts. This has a cost: an amber blip can cost a 3-hourly scheduled fire. Given a 4-hour outage on 09-27, that trade is right. Running cycles hold only at red or unsafe. Amber stops new starts and nothing else.

## (c) hold: why the heartbeat carries the level

Cycles are agents that run SQL over MCP/curl. No shared client exists where a back-off could live in code. The one statement every cycle already runs at every step boundary is the heartbeat UPDATE, so its `RETURNING` carries `db_level` and every step learns the level for free. The heartbeat is also a direct probe: 17–19 s on 09-27 is the rule's "> 10 s". The hold itself is code (`db-pressure.js --hold`: 5 → 10 → 20 → 40 → 80 → 120 s). That replaces the 5–10 s hand retries seen on 09-27. The 60-min cap is there so a held cycle cannot outlive the success bound (no outage > 40 min) by more than one extra period. Past the cap the cycle closes `failed`, and the claim expires by the existing 24 h rule.
